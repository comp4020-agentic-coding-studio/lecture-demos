An interactive explainer

No photon knows the picture. Count enough of them and the photograph is just there.

A camera has no idea what it is looking at. Light arrives at the sensor as separate photons; each one lands in one cell; the cell adds one to a tally. That is the whole of it. A photograph is what a few million tallies look like from far enough away.

Every argument a photographer has ever had follows from that one fact. Aperture is how wide the hole is, so it sets how many photons get through each second. Shutter speed is how long the counting runs. ISO multiplies the tally after the counting has stopped — which is exactly why it can brighten a photograph and can never improve one.

Below is a sensor of 220 × 140 cells with every photon simulated separately. Nothing in it draws a picture, applies a blur, or adds grain. Press the shutter and watch those things happen anyway.

Three ways to double the light. None of them is free, and the sim charges you for each one in a different currency.

Three things to try

1. Just run it

Turn the speed down and watch the first few hundred photons arrive. There is no picture yet — there is a scatter of lit cells, and it is the brightest parts of the scene that get lit first, because that is where photons are most likely to come from. Keep watching. Nothing sharpens, nothing resolves, nothing is applied: the tallies just get bigger, and somewhere around a hundred thousand arrivals the scatter stops looking like a scatter.

2. Take the light away, then buy it back three ways

Switch to Candlelit. Everything in the room loses three stops — everything except the bulb, which is a lamp and keeps its own light, so the meter reads a little better than three stops down. The frame collapses into speckle. Now buy those three stops back: first with ISO alone, then reset and use the aperture, then reset and use the shutter.

ISO gets the brightness back and not one bit of the quality: the grain readout does not move, because the sensor counted exactly as few photons as before and ISO only multiplied them. The aperture gets the brightness back and cleans the frame up — eight times the photons, a third of the grain — and softens the window behind the bulb, because a wider hole gathers each point over a wider disc. The shutter gets the brightness back and the same clean-up, and smears the bulb into a stripe, because the bulb kept moving while the counting ran.

This is the exposure triangle, and it is not a rule of thumb. It is three different physical quantities that happen to have the same effect on one number.

3. Relight the set while it runs

Pick the Shade brush and drag it across the sill, leaving the window alone. Watch what happens to the two halves of the frame. The window stays clean; the sill dissolves into noise — in the same photograph, at the same ISO, with the same sensor. Grain is not a property of the camera. It is a property of how much light a particular part of the scene sent you.

Then take the Light brush to the shadows. They come back, and they come back clean. This is why photographers move lamps around instead of turning ISO up.

Under the hood

What the simulation actually does, so you can check the claims above against it.

  • Scene. Radiance is authored, not rendered: each of the 30,800 cells carries a brightness and a distance from the focal plane. The light brush multiplies a region's brightness; the presets multiply the whole set.
  • Emission. Photons are drawn independently, each one landing on a cell with probability proportional to that cell's radiance. Counts per cell are therefore Poisson, which is what real photon arrivals are. Nothing anywhere adds a noise term.
  • Aperture. The photon budget scales as (4/N)² — the area of the hole. Defocus is geometric: a point at distance d from the focal plane spreads over a disc of radius 13·d/N cells, sampled uniformly. The bulb sits on the focal plane and so is never defocused.
  • Shutter. The budget scales linearly with the open time. Each photon picks its own arrival instant inside that window, and the bulb is wherever the pendulum puts it at that instant. Motion blur is not drawn; it is the spread of those instants.
  • ISO. Applied once, at readout, as a multiplier on the finished tally. It is a parameter of the display function and appears nowhere in the emission or the noise — spec/exposure.test.ts fails if it ever does.
  • Metering. "Balanced" means the scene's mean radiance renders at 18% grey. Grain is reported as 1/√N at that metered tone.

What it leaves out

This sensor is an ideal photon counter, and a real one is not. The gap matters most for the cleanest claim on this page.

ISO is not quite as innocent as the sim makes it look. Shot noise is the only noise modelled here, and ISO genuinely cannot touch it. Real sensors also have read noise, added after the analogue gain on many designs — so on those cameras raising ISO really does buy a slightly cleaner shadow, which is why photographers argue about whether a given body is "ISO-invariant". The claim that survives is the one the sim demonstrates: ISO never recovers a photon you did not collect.

Also absent: dark current, fixed-pattern non-uniformity, quantisation, colour of any kind (there is no colour filter array here, so no colour noise and no demosaic), lens aberrations, and diffraction. That last one is why the sim rewards opening the aperture all the way and a real lens does not.
